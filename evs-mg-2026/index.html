CA 1: World Population Year (1974) & International Women's Year (1975)

Concept Breakthrough This topic tracks the historical shift in global policy making from a mechanical "population control" approach to a human-rights-focused development model that integrates women's empowerment.
1974: World Population Year (Bucharest Conference)

The UN held the first World Population Conference in Bucharest. Western nations initially pushed for strict, top-down quantitative population control targets. However, developing nations (led by India and Argentina) resisted, arguing that "Development is the best contraceptive". The conference formally recognized that population policies cannot be decoupled from socio-economic progress.

1975: International Women's Year (Mexico City Conference)

Centering on "Equality, Development, and Peace", the first World Conference on Women was held in Mexico City. It transformed the global policy perspective on women: rather than treating them as passive recipients of family planning or economic aid, they were recognized as active agents and equal participants in planning and policy making.

Key Policy Impacts

  • Integration of Women: The 1974 Plan of Action recommended increasing women's political, economic, and social roles, which became the cornerstone of the 1975 goals.
  • Reproductive Rights: Replaced strict demographic targets with the autonomy of couples and individuals to choose their family size safely.
  • Long-Term UN Infrastructure: Spawned the UN Decade for Women (1976–1985), the International Research and Training Institute for the Advancement of Women (INSTRAW), and the United Nations Development Fund for Women (UNIFEM).
Memory Aid: The "Autonomy Chain"

Bucharest (1974) shifted focus from Numbers to Development.
Mexico City (1975) shifted focus from Development to Women's Agency.
Together they established that: Healthy Women = Sustainable Demographics.

CA 2: Norman Borlaug & The Green Revolution

Who was Norman Borlaug? An American agronomist (1914–2009) widely hailed as the "Father of the Green Revolution". He won the Nobel Peace Prize in 1970 for saving hundreds of millions of lives through agricultural science.

1. Development of Semi-Dwarf Wheat (Mexico)

Working under the Rockefeller Foundation in Mexico in the 1940s-50s, Borlaug bred semi-dwarf wheat varieties. Traditional tall wheat strains would bend and collapse (known as lodging) under the weight of large, fertilizer-heavy grain heads. Borlaug's shorter, sturdier wheat could absorb abundant nutrients without falling, drastically raising crop yields and making Mexico wheat self-sufficient by 1956.

He pioneered "shuttle breeding"—growing two crops a year in geographically distinct Mexican regions (one in highland Chapingo, one in lowland Sonora)—slashing the time needed to breed new varieties in half and producing crops with wide ecological tolerance.

2. Deployment to South Asia (India & Pakistan)

In the mid-1960s, during severe food crisis/famines in India and Pakistan, Borlaug imported tons of semi-dwarf wheat seed (Lerma Rojo 64 and Sonora 64). Under partnerships with scientists like M.S. Swaminathan in India, grain yields doubled in just a few seasons, causing a production boom termed the Green Revolution.

Critical Evaluation for Exams Be ready to discuss the dual legacy of the Green Revolution:
  • Pros: Massive reduction in starvation, grain self-sufficiency for vulnerable nations, preservation of forests by increasing crop yields on existing farmland.
  • Cons: Heavy reliance on expensive chemical inputs (synthetic fertilizers and pesticides), water tables depleted by intensive tubewell irrigation, loss of crop genetic diversity, and economic marginalization of smallholders.

Biodiversity Course Book Summary (Upto Section 4.8 / Page 94)

Section 4.1 Introduction & Definitions

Biodiversity is defined as the variety of life on Earth across three levels of biological organization:

  • Genetic Diversity: The variation of genes within a single species. (e.g., thousands of distinct traditional cultivars of rice like basmati, jasmine). It allows species to adapt to environmental changes and resist diseases.
  • Species Diversity: The variety of different species within a region. Estimated to be around 1.8 million species described worldwide, but true numbers could be 10 million or more.
  • Ecosystem Diversity: The variety of ecological habitats, niches, and communities (e.g., tropical rainforests, coral reefs, grasslands, wetlands, mangroves).
Section 4.2 Biogeographic Classification of India

India is divided into 10 distinct Biogeographic Zones based on climate, terrain, and characteristic vegetation patterns:

  1. Trans-Himalaya: Cold, high-altitude desert of Ladakh.
  2. Himalaya: Rugged mountain ranges and valleys.
  3. Terai: Wet swampy grasslands at the Himalayan foothills.
  4. Gangetic & Brahmaputra Plains: Highly fertile alluvial plains.
  5. Thar Desert: Arid sand dune ecosystem of Rajasthan.
  1. Deccan Peninsula (Semi-arid): Dry forest/grasslands plateau.
  2. Northeast States: High-rainfall tropical forest zone.
  3. Western Ghats: Mountain range with rich evergreen forests.
  4. Andaman & Nicobar Islands: Equatorial island biodiversity.
  5. Coastal Belts: Mangroves, sandy beaches, estuaries.
Section 4.3 Value of Biodiversity

Biodiversity provides irreplaceable services and values categorized as follows:

  • Consumptive Use Value: Direct consumption of natural products (e.g., harvesting firewood, wild fruits, game, forest medicinal herbs) without commercial marketing.
  • Productive Use Value: Commercial products sold in markets (e.g., timber, fish, industrial oils, crops, commercial pharmaceuticals like Taxol from Pacific Yew or Vinblastine from Periwinkle).
  • Social & Ethical Values: Cultural, spiritual, and religious ties to nature (e.g., sacred groves, worship of specific trees/animals like Peepal or Tulsi), and the ethical belief that all species have a right to exist.
  • Aesthetic & Option Values: Ecotourism and recreation; plus option value—preserving species for future potential use (e.g., undiscovered genetic crop properties or drugs).
Section 4.5 India as a Mega-Diversity Nation

India is one of the top 10-15 megadiverse countries. Its unique biodiversity arose because it split from Gondwanaland ~70M years ago, drifted north across the equator, closed the Tethys Sea, collided with Eurasia (importing European/East Asian flora & fauna), and subsequently received savanna species from Africa.

Section 4.6 Hotspots of Biodiversity

Biodiversity hotspots are biogeographic regions with significant reservoir of biodiversity that is under threat from humans. Key acceptances in India:

  • Northeast Forests: Extremely rich in species, including 63% of land mammal genera in India and 1,500 endemic plant species.
  • Western Ghats: Rich in amphibians and reptiles, particularly snakes, plus 1,500 endemic plant species.
  • Andaman & Nicobar: Isolated island ecosystem with 2,200 flowering plants and high endemism.
Section 4.7 Threats to Biodiversity

Major human-driven threats leading to species extinction include:

  • Habitat Loss: Conversion of forests to agriculture/plantations, clearing mangroves for firewood/shrimp farming, draining wetlands.
  • Exotic Invasive Weeds: *Lantana*, *Eupatorium*, and *Parthenium* (Congress grass) spreading at the expense of native flora.
  • Poaching & Wildlife Trade: Tiger skin/bones, elephant ivory, rhino horn, musk deer scent, bear gall bladders, marine shells/corals.
Case Study 1: Kailadevi Wildlife Sanctuary (Rajasthan)

Initiated in 1983 as part of Ranthambore Tiger Reserve. Local Meena and Gujjar pastoralist communities faced pressure from illegal mining, timber extraction, and migrant Rabari grazers (herding 150,000 sheep). In 1990, the villagers formed the "Baragaon ki Panchayat" and launched the "Bhed Bhagao Andolan". Supported by the Forest Department, they created Forest Protection Committees to check illegal logging and ban mining, successfully managing their own resources.

Case Study 2: Kokkare Bellur Village (Karnataka)

A village in Karnataka representing an outstanding example of man-wildlife co-existence. Endangered spot-billed pelicans and painted storks migrate here every December to nest on tall tamarind trees in the village center. Villagers protect these birds, viewing them as symbols of good luck for rain. In return, they harvest guano (nitrate-rich bird droppings) from deep pits dug under the nesting trees, mixing it with silt to use and sell as a powerful natural fertilizer.
